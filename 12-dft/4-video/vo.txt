A customer calls support, hears "press one for billing", and presses a key. Your line never sees a key, only samples. The beep sounds like one note, and it's two: a low tone for the key's row, a high tone for its column. Key five is seven seventy hertz plus thirteen thirty-six. Which two?
Start smaller. Eight samples of a two thousand hertz tone: one, zero, minus one, zero. Multiply them by your own copy, a test tone, and add up. A two thousand hertz test scores four. One thousand hertz scores exactly zero.
Now a nasty surprise. The same tone, a quarter turn behind, scores zero. So take a backward wheel, turning clockwise, test with both its shadows, left-right and up-down, and treat the two sums as an arrow. Its length is four, whatever the phase. That length is the match.
Why? Remember lesson six: a sample is two half arrows laid tip to tail, one turning forward, its mirror turning backward. Now lay each sample along a test wheel, turning backward, clockwise, at the test tone's speed.
The forward half stands still, like a fan under a strobe, and its arrows pile up: N over two, which is four in a block of eight. The mirror half turns twice as fast, steps back and forth, and ends where it started.
Against the one thousand hertz wheel, the arrows close an octagon and end where they began: exactly zero. Any whole number of turns of difference does the same. So test only with whole-turn wheels. Each one is a bin: bin k makes k turns in a block of N samples. The list of their matches is the DFT.
Eight samples put the bins a thousand hertz apart, so every row tone lands in bin one. That spacing, eight thousand over N, is the frequency resolution. Close tones need a longer block. At forty samples, seven seventy and eight fifty-two share a bin. At two hundred and five, each tone gets its own bin, and an empty one on each side.
A customer presses five. Two hundred and five samples, eight keypad bins. Bin twenty scores ninety percent of a full match, bin thirty-four ninety-two, the other six at thirteen percent or less. [surprise-oh] Row seven seventy, column thirteen thirty-six: the key five.
But silence has a strongest bin too. So give the detector a bar at twenty-five percent: a key counts only when exactly one row and one column clear it. Your turn, in the lab. Start at forty samples and watch keys five and eight collide. Then lengthen the block until they come apart, and decode a customer's account number.
[sigh] Then billing complains: some voices fake a key. A real press has almost nothing outside its two tones, so the detector must check every bin. That costs one point six five million multiplications a second, and the cheap chip manages about a million. That's the next bug.
