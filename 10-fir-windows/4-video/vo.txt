Instead, a ghost glides down to where voices live, swelling and fading: nine percent at thirty-five hundred hertz, five at three thousand, two at one thousand. And voices near thirty-five hundred come out louder. How can a filter that only multiplies and adds make anything louder?
Start from the perfect filter, the ideal low-pass: below four thousand hertz everything passes, above it nothing. With no feedback, a filter is just a list of coefficients. Send in one click, and the list comes back out. But the ideal list never ends.
So lesson five kept the middle thirty-one and threw the rest away. That's truncation. At zero hertz the gain is the sum. Point five. Add a pair: one point one four. Another: point nine two. It overshoots, and swings around one.
Each pair adds an arrow and its mirror, which leaves a plain number, sometimes negative. At forty-five hundred hertz, every pair pulls down, to minus point oh nine. Nine percent of that tone gets out, upside down, and lands at thirty-five hundred.
So keep more coefficients? Sixty-three. Two hundred fifty-five. The bump slides toward the cliff and narrows, but it stays nine percent tall. That's the Gibbs phenomenon. Why won't it shrink?
Cutting the list multiplies it by a window: one for each coefficient you keep, zero for the rest. A rectangle. And a window has its own response: a tall hump, with side lobes from its sharp edges.
The cut filter's response is the step, smeared by that hump: slide it along, and add up what's still on the pass side. When the hump touches the cliff, the lobes past it are left out, and they add up to minus nine percent. [surprise-oh] So a hundred and nine is left. A longer rectangle just narrows the lobes, so the share stays.
The cure isn't a longer window, but one without sharp edges: a taper. The Hamming window fades from one in the middle to almost zero at the ends. Its side lobes almost vanish, but its hump is twice as wide: the cliff becomes a slope.
