# Storyboard: The Ghost in the Sweep

**Problem:** lesson 5's ready-made anti-aliasing filter (16,000 to 8,000 samples/s) is the ideal low-pass cut to 31 coefficients. A test sweep from 4,000 to 8,000 Hz should come out silent, but a ghost glides down into the voice band, swelling to 9% at 3,500 Hz, 5% at 3,000 Hz and 2% at 1,000 Hz, and voices near 3,500 Hz come out 109% loud. **Audience:** software engineers with no signal processing background who did lessons 1 to 9. **Goal:** the viewer can say that the ideal low-pass's coefficient list never ends, so the filter keeps the middle 31 (truncation); that at 0 Hz the gain is the sum and it overshoots (0.500, 1.137, 0.924, ... 0.980); that each pair adds an arrow and its mirror, a plain number that can be negative, so 4,500 Hz comes out at −0.09 and is heard at 3,500 Hz; that more coefficients only narrow the bump, which stays at 1.09 (the Gibbs phenomenon); that cutting is multiplying by a rectangle window whose own response has side lobes (first −22%); that the cut response is the step smeared by that hump, and the lobes left out past the cliff add up to −9%, so 109% is left at any length; that a taper (Hamming) has almost no side lobes but a hump twice as wide, so the cliff becomes a slope; and that the slope lands in the empty 3,400 to 4,000 Hz margin, so Hamming 45 meets the job (0.24% on both bands) on a chip that affords 47, where the plain cut needs 271. **Emotion:** from "every tone should be removed" to the surprise that the 9% is a fixed share of the hump's area, then the calm of a fix that trades the cliff for a slope.
