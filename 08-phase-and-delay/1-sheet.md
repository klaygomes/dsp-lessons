# The Fax That Smears

Objective: after this, you can tell a tone's phase from its group delay, read group delay as the slope of the phase, recognize linear phase (symmetric coefficients, equal delay for every tone), build an all-pass that evens a filter's delays without changing any gain, and explain why a minimum-phase filter can't be made earlier.

Bug: a fax modem using the band 1,000 to 1,800 Hz fails on the line. It needs every tone in its band to arrive within 1.5 samples (0.19 ms) of every other, stated in the opening. The symptom is bursts shuffled in time: those near the top of the band arrive about 3 samples late and overlap the next burst. Every tone passes (98.7% down to 70.1%), but lesson 7's notch, y[n] = 0.861 (x[n] + x[n−2]) − 0.7225 y[n−2] (poles at radius 0.85), gives the band group delays from 0.31 to 3.24 samples, a spread of 2.92 samples (0.37 ms).
Fix: act 2 of the lab: add a second-order all-pass after the notch and drag its pole until the group-delay curve over 1,000 to 1,800 Hz fits a 1.5-sample window. Reference solution: pole radius 0.65 at 54° (1,200 Hz), spread 0.95 samples (4.72 near 1,600 Hz to 5.67 at 1,800 Hz). The other fix (a symmetric notch of about 65 coefficients, 4 ms delay) is told in prose, not built.

Key words: phase (not "lag", "offset", "phase shift"; "trails" and "sets back" as verbs, lesson 3's wording; "older is clockwise"), gain (not "attenuation", "level"), group delay (not "envelope delay", "time delay"), the swell (the two-tone beat's shape; not "beat" or "envelope" once named), burst (the fax's unit of sound; not "symbol", "pulse", "beep"), the smear (late bursts overlapping their neighbors), the window (the fax's 1.5-sample tolerance), steepness / slope (of the phase curve; "derivative" only inside the optional box), linear phase, all-pass (not "phase equalizer"), minimum phase, tone, turn, the wheel and the dot, unit circle, pole, zero, notch, passes, stable.

Terms (new, as written in the prose):
- linear phase: a filter whose phase grows in proportion to the tone; that's the same as saying it delays every tone by the same number of samples.
- group delay: the group delay of a filter at one tone is how many samples late a burst made of tones near it arrives; it equals how steeply the phase climbs there, the phase difference between two very close tones divided by their turn difference.
- all-pass: a filter that passes every tone at 100% and changes only its phase; each of its poles sits inside the unit circle with a zero on the same line from the center, at one over the pole's radius, outside.
- minimum phase: a filter whose poles and zeros all sit inside the unit circle; of all the filters that give every tone the same gain, it delays the least.

Reused, with the earlier wording: a tone is a dot turning counter-clockwise, 360° × tone ÷ 8,000 per sample (lesson 3); older copies sit clockwise behind (series convention); gain: how much a system scales one tone, output size divided by input size (lesson 3); phase: how many degrees the output's dot trails the input's (lesson 3); radians with π meaning 4,000 Hz (lesson 6, only in the optional box); the mirror arrow (lesson 6); filter, passes, unit circle, zero, pole, notch, stable, lesson 6's distance rule (lesson 6); the r = 0.85 notch (lesson 7). The book is named in full in the optional box and again in the main text at minimum phase: Oppenheim and Schafer, Discrete-Time Signal Processing (1989), chapter 5. The edge case (zeros on the circle) is explained: such a zero is its own flip.

Misconception: "Undo the delay: build the opposite filter, which speeds up the late tones." · Why wrong: the inverse of an all-pass turns its outside zero into a pole outside the unit circle, which is unstable; a live line can't play a tone before it arrives, so delay can only be evened by adding it to the early tones.
Secondary (taught in worked example 2): "a tone's delay is phase ÷ turn" holds for the wave and fails for the burst (0.56 against 3.24 samples at 1,800 Hz).

Worked examples:
1. Phase tables. Pure 3-sample delay: 67.5°, 135°, 270° at 500, 1,000, 2,000 Hz, phase ÷ turn = 3. Two-point average: 11.25°, 22.5°, 33.75°, 45°, 67.5° at 500 to 3,000 Hz, 0.5 for all. The notch: 9.2°, 13.6°, 21.3°, 29.7°, 39.0°, 45.5° at 1,000, 1,250, 1,500, 1,650, 1,750, 1,800 Hz; phase ÷ turn 0.20 to 0.56.
2. Two tones 1,700 + 1,800 Hz: swell every 80 samples, faster dot gains 4.5° per sample, dots aligned at sample 1,600 (the 20th swell). Notch phases 33.9° and 45.5°: waves late 0.44 and 0.56 samples, swell late 11.6 ÷ 4.5 = 2.58 samples (checked by running the filter). Far tones 1,000 + 1,100 Hz: phases 9.2° and 10.7°, swell late 0.34.

Derivation: main text gives one idea (phase difference ÷ turn difference, the secant settling onto the curve's steepness) with the group-delay figure. The optional box: two dots line up when ω₂n − φ₂ = ω₁n − φ₁, so τ = (φ₂ − φ₁)/(ω₂ − ω₁), and in the limit τ_g = dφ/dω (the book writes −d/dω arg H(e^{jω})); pure delay φ = Dω gives τ_g = D. The all-pass ratio is shown by similar triangles (center, pole, tone) and (center, tone, zero): sides 0.65 : 1 and 1 : 1.54, shared angle, so the distances are in ratio 1.54. No conjugates, no cosine sum rule.

Assumptions:
- Lesson 3 defined gain, phase and frequency response with the wording above; this lesson avoids "lag".
- Lesson 7's fix left the notch at radius 0.85 (its example value, inside its accepted 0.816 to 0.866).
- The fax's band (1,000 to 1,800 Hz) and its 1.5-sample window are story facts of this world, not a fax standard; real high-speed modems are limited by group-delay spread the same way, at other tolerances.
- That a tone switched on and off "is made of" neighboring tones is stated, not proved; the DFT lessons prove it.
- Zeros on the unit circle count as the edge case of minimum phase, as the book allows.
- The design of a long symmetric notch is left to lesson 10.
