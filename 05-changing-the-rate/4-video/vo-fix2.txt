Lesson three's wheel. A tone is a turning dot, and a sample is its left-right shadow. Twice the samples, half the turn. One thousand hertz turns twenty-two and a half degrees. Seven thousand turns one hundred fifty-seven and a half, under half a turn, so here it's a real tone.
Why? Each step of the seven thousand dot is a flip, a half turn, then twenty-two and a half degrees back. On even samples the flips pair up into whole turns, so it sits at the hum's mirror spot, the same shadow. On odd samples one flip is left, so the shadow is opposite.
After the zeros, the bridge needs a low-pass filter, gain two below four thousand to undo the halving, and zero above. Averaging a gap's two neighbors slopes too gently. A longer rule steps more steeply. Thirty-one samples keeps the voice, and every image under eight percent.
Going down, the bridge keeps every other sample. That's downsampling. The hiss of an S, at six thousand hertz, lands on your line as a two thousand hertz squeal, an alias across the same mirror. So the low-pass filter goes first, gain one, while six thousand is still six thousand.
Upsampling never adds detail. The best bridge adds almost nothing above four thousand, and ours leaves a faint seven percent. [sigh] Then a new ticket. A thin whine on every call, exactly four thousand hertz, on the mirror line.
