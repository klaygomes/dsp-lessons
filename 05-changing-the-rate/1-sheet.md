# Ghosts on the Wideband Line

Objective: after this, you can predict the image that upsampling by 2 makes of any tone (8,000 Hz minus the tone, both at half height), predict the alias that downsampling by 2 makes of any tone above 4,000 Hz, and place one low-pass filter correctly in each direction: after the zeros going up, before dropping samples going down.

Bug: the bridge to a 16,000 samples/s wideband line puts a ghost copy of every voice above 4,000 Hz; a 1,000 Hz hum also arrives at 7,000 Hz, and the ghost moves down when the voice moves up.
Fix: act 2 of the lab: going up, pick the shortest rule after the zeros that keeps 300 to 3,400 Hz between 90% and 110% and every image under 8% (the 31-sample rule); going down, place the same step with gain 1 before dropping samples so no "s" lands on the line as a squeal. A ripple of about 7% leftover image remains, the seed of lesson 10.

Key words: upsample (not "interpolate", "resample" or "stretch"), downsample (not "decimate"), image (not "ghost" after the term is defined, except as the customers' word), the mirror (the line at 4,000 Hz), the bridge (the converter), rule (for the weighted sums; "filter" only in the phrase "low-pass filter", as lesson 4 used it), tone, gain, alias, the limit, wideband line, your line, frequency meter.

Terms:
- upsample: to upsample by 2 is to put one zero between every two samples, so 8,000 samples per second become 16,000.
- downsample: to downsample by 2 is to keep every other sample and drop the rest, so 16,000 samples per second become 8,000.
- image: a copy of a tone that upsampling creates at the mirror pitch, 8,000 Hz minus the tone.

Reused from earlier lessons (wording kept): sample, sampling rate, the limit, alias (lesson 4); tone as a wheel, "a tone is a dot turning 360° × tone ÷ 8,000 per sample", frequency response, gain (lesson 3); impulse response, convolution (lesson 2); linear (lesson 1). The wheel is extended once, explicitly, to 16,000/s. The prose adds one bridge sentence: the dot's left-right position at angle θ is cos θ.

Misconception: "Upsampling adds detail: more samples per second make the call richer, wideband." · Why wrong: nothing above 4,000 Hz was ever stored, so all upsampling can put above 4,000 Hz is images, and the best bridge puts none.

Worked examples:
1. 1,000 Hz hum (1, 0.71, 0, −0.71, −1, ...) upsampled by 2: (1, 0, 0.71, 0, 0, 0, −0.71, 0, −1), which equals half of 1,000 Hz (22.5° per sample) plus half of 7,000 Hz (157.5° per sample) at 16,000/s, sample for sample: equal on even samples, opposite on odd. Wheel reason: on even samples the 7,000 Hz dot is turning 22.5° backwards (same shadow); on odd samples one flip is left over (opposite shadow).
2. A 6,000 Hz "s" at 16,000/s (135° per sample: 1, −0.71, 0, 0.71, −1, ...), downsampled by 2: kept samples 1, 0, −1, 0, ... are a 2,000 Hz tone on your line (270° forwards per kept sample, same as 90° backwards), at full height.

Derivation: the main line is the wheel argument by position (at sample m the 7,000 Hz dot sits at m flips plus −22.5° × m, so even m gives the mirror angle and the same shadow, odd m the opposite shadow; for any φ the image turns 180° − φ, which is 8,000 Hz minus the tone), plus the gain of the neighbor fill, 1 + cos φ, from cos(a − b) + cos(a + b) = 2 cos a cos b. The book's own treatment (Oppenheim and Schafer, first edition 1989, chapter 3, changing the sampling rate by an integer factor, argued with the frequency response) appears only as an optional box: x_e[m] = x[m/2] on even m, 0 on odd; φ = θ/2; the switch (1 + (−1)^m)/2; (−1)^m cos(φm) = cos((180° − φ)m). Downsampling x_d[n] = x[2n] doubles the turn. L = 3 is shown by a plain table, and L − 1 images at 1/L height, gain L, stated from it.
