# The Whine on Line Two

Objective: after this, you can design a filter that removes one tone, by placing zeros on the unit circle at the tone's angle and poles just inside beside them, and you can tell whether a filter is stable from where its poles sit. Along the way you can read a turn in radians, multiply by j as a quarter turn, and split a real tone into its arrow and its mirror arrow.

Bug: calls carry a whine, first at 4,000 Hz (a leaking clock chip, on lesson 5's mirror line, inside the samples, so lesson 4's filter before the sampler can't reach it), then at 2,000 Hz (the replacement chip).
Fix: act 2 of the lab: drag the zero pair onto a whine at a random frequency, then move the poles out from the center toward the rim to keep the voice above 90% without passing radius 1.

Key words: filter (not "system" in prose; "system function" only as the book's name for H(z); "low-pass filter" as lessons 4 and 5 used it), passes (not "lets through" in labels), removes (not "kills"; "cancels" only for the sample arithmetic), the whine, the voice, zero, pole, feedback, unit circle, notch, stable, mirror arrow (not "conjugate" in prose), radians, j (not "imaginary unit" except once, in quotes).

Terms (new in this lesson):
- filter: a rule that builds each output sample from input samples, and with feedback also from its own earlier outputs.
- passes / removes: how much of a tone a filter passes is its gain, as a percentage; a tone that gets out at 0% is removed.
- radians: a turn measured as the walk along the rim of a radius-1 wheel; a full turn is 2π. ω = 2π × tone ÷ 8,000, so π means 4,000 Hz.
- j: a quarter turn counter-clockwise, so j × j = −1. The point at angle θ is cos θ + j sin θ, written e^{jθ}; e^{jωn} is the tone's dot.
- unit circle: one point per tone, 0 Hz at the right, 4,000 Hz at the left, the bottom half holding each tone's mirror arrow.
- mirror arrow: a real tone is two half-size arrows turning opposite ways; the clockwise one, on the bottom half, is the mirror arrow.
- zero: a point where the filter's output vanishes; tones on it are removed.
- feedback: the filter reusing its own earlier output samples.
- pole: a point where the response shoots up; comes from feedback; its radius is how much an echo keeps per sample.
- notch: a filter that removes one narrow band of tones and passes the rest.
- stable: every pole strictly inside the unit circle, so echoes die out.
- system function H(z): the number a tone z^n is multiplied by when it passes through the filter.

Reused from earlier lessons (wording kept, not re-explained): sample (1); impulse response, convolution (2); tone as a wheel, "a dot turning 360° × tone ÷ 8,000 per sample, whose left-right position is the sample", counter-clockwise, older is clockwise, frequency response, gain, phase (3); the limit, alias, the backwards dot with the same shadow, lesson 4's filter before the sampler (4); image, the mirror line at 4,000 Hz, low-pass filter, weighted-sum rules (5). The wheel section is a short recap with lesson 3's wording, not a first introduction.

Misconception: "The filter detects the whine and subtracts it." Wrong because a filter treats every tone by its point on the circle, whether a whine is present or not: a sung 2,000 Hz note is removed too.

Worked examples:
1. Two-point average (impulse response ½, ½) on the 4,000 Hz whine (+1, −1, ..., the chip's tick peaks on the sample moments; no knife-edge aside): every average is 0. On 500 Hz: the average of the fresh and one-sample-old dots lands 0.98 from the center, 11.25° behind (half a sample): 98% passes.
2. 2,000 Hz whine (1, 0, −1, 0, ...): the average passes 71%; (x[n] + x[n−2]) / 2 gives 0 everywhere (zeros at ±j).

Rule (one rule for every case): multiply the distances to the zeros, divide by the distances to the poles, compare with the same number for 0 Hz.

Story: after the average ships, a few tickets say "a bit muffled, but much better" (it keeps 56% at 2,500 Hz, 38% at 3,000 Hz); customers live with it, so the later "sock" ticket is the two-back filter biting into the middle of the voice, not a first sign of dullness.

Where each new tool enters (no separate tools section): radians in "A circle that holds every tone", labeling the circle; j in "The whine moves", to name the top point; "Multiplying points" (sizes multiply, angles add, (0.71 + 0.71j)² = j, (0.9j)² = −0.81) opens the derivation; e^{jθ} is step one, with θ (any angle) told apart from ω, and z named in words ("the point one sample's turn reaches") before its formula. The zero pair (z² = −1 gives ±j) and the general zero pair at θ moved out of the derivation into "The voice gets dull".

Derivation (Oppenheim and Schafer, *Discrete-Time Signal Processing*, first edition 1989, chapter 4), now six steps after "Multiplying points": (1) e^{jθ} = cos θ + j sin θ, x[n] = z^n; (2) delay = z^{-1}, a turn of ω clockwise; a minus in the exponent means clockwise; (3) H(z) = ½(1 + z^{-1}); its size is the gain, its angle is how far the output dot sits behind (lesson 3's phase, clockwise as minus); 500 Hz: 0.98 at −11.25°; (4) zero at z = −1 (ω = π); (5) | | defined as size, |a − b| as distance, sizes multiply; |H| = distance ÷ 2; (6) cos(ωn) = ½e^{jωn} + ½e^{−jωn}; linearity (lesson 1) means each arrow is multiplied by H at its own point, so a zero on one arrow removes only that half. In "The voice gets dull": 1 − p z^{-1} vanishes at z = p; the pair multiplies out to 1 − 2cos θ z^{-1} + z^{-2} using step six's identity e^{jθ} + e^{−jθ} = 2cos θ. The feedback program says at first sight that 0.81 = 0.9 × 0.9 and 0.905 sets the volume, explained after the factoring. Then "Off the rim": H(z) can be evaluated anywhere; tones only visit |z| = 1; (0.9j)^n is a 2,000 Hz turn shrinking by 0.9. Poles derived like zeros: 1 + 0.81z^{-2} = (1 − 0.9j z^{-1})(1 + 0.9j z^{-1}), so H = 0.905 (z − j)(z + j) / ((z − 0.9j)(z + 0.9j)), each factor a distance.
