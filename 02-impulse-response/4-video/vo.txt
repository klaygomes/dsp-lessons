And the box passes lesson one's two tests. Doubling the input doubles the echo, and two inputs added give their echoes added, so it's linear. Delaying the input only delays the echo, so it's time-invariant.
So what's the simplest thing you could send? Not a word, and not a tone. A single click: one sample at one, and every other sample at zero. That signal is called an impulse.
Send it into the echo path and record what comes back. That's the impulse response, written h. Silence for two thousand samples, then point four, then point two, then silence for good. Can two sizes and a delay really predict the echo of a whole sentence?
Try a very short word. A caller's hi lasts four samples: point eight, point four, minus point six, minus point two. You only know what the box does to a click, so turn the word into clicks: each sample is a click of its own size, at its own place. Add the four clicks, and you get the word back.
Now each click launches its own copy of h. Linearity scales it by the click's size. Time-invariance starts it as many samples late as its click. Add the copies, sample by sample, and out comes the echo: five samples, starting at two thousand, peaking at point three two. [surprise-oh] One click just predicted the echo of a word you never sent.
That's convolution: building the output as a sum of shifted copies of the impulse response, each scaled by one input sample. The book writes it x star h, and that star is not multiplication. Multiply hi by h sample by sample, and every product has a zero in it. No echo at all.
Here's the fix. Your equipment saw every sample of the caller's voice, and you measured h. So compute the echo yourself: point four times the voice two thousand samples back, plus point two times the voice two thousand and one samples back. Subtract that guess from what arrives. The echo is gone, and the far voice comes through at full size.
