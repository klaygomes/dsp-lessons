# Your Own Voice, a Quarter Second Late

Objective: after this, you can measure an LTI system's impulse response with one click, compute its output for any input by convolution (both by rows of shifted copies and by one output sample at a time), and build an echo canceller that subtracts x * h.

Bug: callers hear their own voice come back 0.25 s (2,000 samples) later. The far-end box, where the digital line meets the copper wire, leaks part of the voice back: h[2000] = 0.4, h[2001] = 0.2.
Fix: act 2 of the lab: on today's live call (rerouted, and the box has drifted, so its h is not the textbook one: D = 2,012, a = 0.34, b = 0.17, never printed in the prose), in a quiet moment send a click of 0.1, divide what comes back by 0.1, read the delay and the two sizes, set the canceller echo_guess[n] = a voice[n − D] + b voice[n − D − 1], and subtract it from the earpiece; the echo meter reads 0%. The canceller stays locked until a clean click is measured. b/a = 0.5 as in the textbook h, so a one-sample slip still leaves 133% on "hi" and about 25% on the call.

Key words: impulse (not "delta function", "spike", "pulse"; "click" is the everyday picture of it and is used alongside), impulse response (not "kernel", "transfer function", "echo profile"), convolution / convolve (not "filtering", "correlation", "mixing"), copy (a shifted, scaled copy of h), the echo, the echo path, the echo canceller, the far voice, delay, size (not "gain": lesson 3's term). Reused from lesson 1: signal, system, linear, time-invariant, LTI, sample, tone, the booster, the smoother.

Terms:
- impulse: a signal that is 1 at n = 0 and 0 everywhere else, written δ[n].
- impulse response: what a system gives back when the input is the impulse, written h[n].
- convolution: building the output as a sum of shifted copies of the impulse response, each scaled by one input sample: y[n] = Σ_k x[k] h[n − k], written x[n] * h[n].
- Helper words, not glossary terms: caller and far person (the cast; your equipment sits at the caller's end), echo canceller (a system that computes x * h and subtracts it), the far voice, echo meter (sum of squares of the leftover compared with the echo alone).
- k in the sum runs over all whole numbers; only terms where x[k] and h[n − k] are both nonzero count. Σ is read as "add up". The loop form is in the prose.
- One convention for h: the full list h[0] to h[2001], 2,002 numbers (2,000 zeros, then 0.4, 0.2). "First nonzero number" is used whenever only the 0.4, 0.2 part is meant. The canceller code uses the delay-plus-sizes form, described as skipping the zeros.
- T, x, y: restated from lesson 1 in "What we know about the echo path": the system is T, the input x, the output y, y[n] = T{x[n]}.

Misconception: "Convolution multiplies the input by the impulse response, sample by sample: x[n] × h[n]." (The book's star invites it, and in NumPy `x * h` is exactly that; convolution is `np.convolve`.) Why wrong: "hi" lives at n = 0 to 3 and h at 2,000 to 2,001, so every product is 0 and it predicts no echo at all; really each input sample launches a whole shifted copy of h.

Worked examples:
1. Echo of "hi" = 0.8, 0.4, −0.6, −0.2 through h = 0.4 at 2,000, 0.2 at 2,001. Copies: 0.32, 0.16 / 0.16, 0.08 / −0.24, −0.12 / −0.08, −0.04. Sum at n = 2,000 to 2,005: 0.32, 0.32, −0.16, −0.20, −0.04, 0. As lists, 4 + 2,002 − 1 = 2,005 numbers, the first 2,000 silent, so 5 sound. Column check shown as a k / x[k] / h[2002 − k] / product table: products 0, 0.08, −0.24, 0, sum −0.16.
2. The smoother from lesson 1 (restated in the prose): click in gives h = 0.5, 0.5. Convolved with the 1,000 Hz tone (restated: repeats every 8 samples, 45° per sample, silent before n = 0), shown to three places: 0.500, 0.854, 0.354, −0.354, −0.854, −0.854, −0.354, 0.354, 0.854, matching lesson 1's two-place row from n = 1 and filling the n = 0 blank lesson 1 left. Counter-case: the ×4 clipping booster answers a click of 1 with 1.0, so convolution predicts 0.1 for a click of 0.1; it really gives 0.4.

Derivation (chapter 2, shortened to one paragraph beside the pile-of-clicks figure): x[n] = Σ x[k] δ[n − k]; Σ x[k] T{δ[n − k]}; time-invariance gives T{δ[n − k]} = h[n − k]; so y = Σ x[k] h[n − k] = x * h. Then renaming the loop variable, m = n − k (count positions in h instead of x), shows x * h = h * x, which reads the echo as 0.4 × voice delayed 2,000 plus 0.2 × voice delayed 2,001.

Deeper idea: earpiece = far voice + x * h, all at the caller's end where your equipment sees x; subtracting x * h needs linearity and a box that does not change while the measurement is used. One measure throughout: sum of squares of the leftover, compared with the echo alone (the lab's echo meter). Sizes 10% low: every leftover sample is a tenth of the echo's, meter 1%. Delay off by one: depends on how sharp the sound is. On "hi" (a zigzag) 133%; on the steady 1,000 Hz tone 59%; for a steady tone at f the leftover is 2 − 2 cos(360° × f ÷ 8,000) of the echo: 300 Hz 5.5%, 1,000 Hz 59%, 2,000 Hz 200%, 3,400 Hz 378%. A delay 20 samples off swings between 0% and 400% across the voice band, about 200% on average. Rule taught: small delay errors hurt the sharp parts of a voice most; large ones ruin it everywhere; so read the delay off a click. Measuring h: send the click in a quiet moment (start of a call or a pause), because the far voice would land on top of the recording; the lab's line is quiet, so its measurement is exact.

Assumptions:
- Lesson 1 taught signal, system, linear, time-invariant (and used the words sample, tone, LTI, the booster, the smoother, the neighbor rule). This lesson reuses them with lesson 1's wording; the smoother and the clipping booster return as examples.
- The echo path's impulse response is two samples (0.4, 0.2) for pencil arithmetic; real echo paths are longer. The near-end sidetone and the far person's own echo are ignored.
- The canceller is fixed (measured once), not adaptive; adaptive cancellers are outside the book's chapter 2.
- Convolution is shown for finite signals; infinite sums and their convergence (stability of h) wait for later lessons.
