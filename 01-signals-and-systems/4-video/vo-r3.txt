The line went live last week. Quiet callers are fine. Loud callers hear a harsh, raspy buzz. One says his boss sounds like he's yelling through a kazoo. [laughter]
Strange, isn't it? Same code for everybody, and louder should just be louder. The line carries the microphone's voltage, measured eight thousand times a second. Each one is a sample, and the list is a signal.
The simplest sound is a tone. How often it repeats, in hertz, is its pitch. Its samples are the cosine of an angle that turns a fixed step: at one thousand hertz, forty-five degrees. Eight samples make a full turn.
Next comes the booster, a system: one signal in, another out. Its rule: multiply every sample by four. But the chip after it only stores values from minus one to plus one. Hand it two, and it writes down one. That's clipping.
A quiet caller is size point two: their biggest sample. A loud one is point five. Which comes out clean? The quiet one grows to point eight. The loud one would reach two, so its tops are sliced off flat.
Is that just an uglier tone? Try the neighbor rule: in any tone, a sample's two neighbors add up to the middle one times a fixed number, here one point four one four. The clean tone passes. The clipped one fails.
So what is it? [surprise-oh] It's two tones: a one thousand hertz tone, minus a three thousand hertz tone about a sixth its size. That second tone is the buzz. The booster made it, out of nothing.
What did the booster break? A system is linear when doubling the input doubles the output, and two inputs added give their outputs added. The real booster is not linear. It only behaves like the linear times four while its outputs stay inside the range.
Here's the trap. Two quiet voices share a speakerphone. Each alone comes out at point eight, fine. Together, linearity says one point six. The chip writes one, and the pair buzzes. Linearity is a promise about sums.
A system is time-invariant when delaying the input only delays the output. The smoother, averaging each sample with the one before, has both properties. A tone goes in; the same pitch comes out, only resized and shifted. No new tones, ever.
The fix: keep every output inside the range. The loudest input, speakerphones included, is point five. Times two lands right on the edge, no margin. Instead, keep the loudest peak at point eight, a fifth below the edge: that's headroom. Point eight over point five is one point six. The buzz meter reads zero.
Loud callers sound like loud people again; quiet ones peak at point three two, quieter but clean. Then a new ticket: I can hear myself. Every word comes back, faint, a quarter second later. That's the next bug.
