The line went live last week, and the support tickets split into two neat piles. Quiet callers are fine. Loud callers hear a harsh, raspy buzz on top of their voice. One says his boss sounds like he's yelling through a kazoo. [laughter]
Strange, isn't it? The line runs the same code for everybody, and louder should just be louder. So look at what it carries: the microphone's voltage, measured eight thousand times a second. Each measurement is a sample, and a call is a list of numbers.
The simplest sound is a tone, like a tuning fork: a steady wave, and how many times a second it repeats, in hertz, is the pitch you hear. Its samples come from the cosine. For one thousand hertz, the angle steps three hundred sixty times one thousand over eight thousand. That's forty-five degrees each sample, so after eight samples the pattern starts over.
Early in the chain sits the booster. It's a system: a rule that takes in one signal, one list of numbers, and gives back another. Its rule is one line of code: multiply every sample by four. But the chip after it can only store values from minus one to plus one. Hand it two, and it writes down one. That flattening is called clipping.
Take a quiet caller at size point two, so their biggest sample is point two, and a loud one at point five. Which comes out clean? The quiet one grows to point eight and stays inside the range. The loud one would reach two, so its tops are sliced off flat.
Is that just an uglier tone? Try a pencil test. In any tone, a sample's two neighbors add up to the middle sample times a fixed number, here one point four one four. The clean tone passes. The clipped one fails.
So what is it? [surprise-oh] It's two tones. A one thousand hertz tone, minus a three thousand hertz tone a sixth its size, lands exactly on every clipped sample. That second tone is the buzz. Nobody said it. The booster made it, out of nothing.
What did the booster break? A system is linear when doubling the input doubles the output, and two inputs added give their outputs added. The real booster is not linear. It only behaves like the linear times four while its inputs stay inside the range.
And here's the trap. Two quiet voices share a speakerphone. Each alone comes out at point eight, fine. Linearity says together they should give one point six. The chip writes one. Each passed alone, and together they buzz. Linearity is a promise about sums.
Now add one more property. A system is time-invariant when delaying the input only delays the output. Together they make a law: a tone that goes into a linear, time-invariant system comes out at the same pitch, only bigger or smaller, and shifted. No new tones, ever.
So the fix is to keep every input inside the range. The loudest input the booster ever sees, speakerphones included, is point five. Times two lands right on the edge, with no margin. Instead, keep the loudest peak at point eight, a fifth below the edge. That gap is the headroom. Point eight divided by point five is one point six. Now the buzz meter, which measures the tones that weren't in the input, reads zero percent on every caller.
Loud callers sound like loud people again. Then a new ticket arrives: I can hear myself. Every word comes back, faint, a quarter of a second later. That's the next bug.
