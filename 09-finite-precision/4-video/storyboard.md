# Storyboard: The Chip That Hums

**Problem:** the clock chip's whine hops between 1,900, 2,000 and 2,100 Hz, so the team builds three narrow notches (poles at radius 0.98) and multiplies them into one sixth-order rule. It is perfect on the laptop. On a cheap 8-bit chip one click starts a hum that grows every sample, hits the chip's limits from sample 76 and swings between them forever. **Audience:** software engineers with no signal processing background who did lessons 1 to 8. **Goal:** the viewer can say that a coefficient is a fixed number a sample is multiplied by; that the 8-bit chip stores each one as a whole number of steps (256 choices) and quantization rounds to the nearest step; that lesson 6's notch survives (step 1/128, 0.81 becomes 104/128 = 0.8125, poles 0.9 to 0.9014); that the three-notch rule's biggest coefficient (2.8576) forces the step to 1/32 for every coefficient, errors about a hundredth (−0.0138, +0.0056, −0.0108); that the rounded rule's poles land at 0.8378 (middle, in) and 1.0567 at 1,817 and 2,183 Hz (outside, the hum); that a pole moves by the error divided by the product of its distances to the other poles, so the crowded middle pole has a multiplier of 22 (0.077 × 0.077 × 1.96³ = 0.045) where lesson 6's lone pair has 0.56; and that a cascade of three second-order sections, each rounded alone at step 1/64, keeps every pole at 0.9763 (whine 1.3%, 0%, 1.3%, voice at 1,500 Hz 99.5%). **Emotion:** from "same rule, same numbers" to the surprise that a hundredth of error is multiplied twenty-two times by crowding, then the calm of building one notch at a time.
