The clock chip's whine has learned to hop, between nineteen hundred, two thousand and twenty-one hundred hertz. So the team builds three narrow notches, one per whine, and multiplies them into one rule.
On the laptop it's perfect. On a cheap eight-bit chip, one click starts a hum that grows every sample, slams into the chip's limits, and swings between them forever. Same rule, same numbers. Or are they?
Every number in that rule is a coefficient, a fixed number a sample gets multiplied by. The chip stores each one in eight bits: a whole number of steps, two hundred fifty-six choices. Rounding to the nearest step is called quantization.
Try lesson six's notch first. Its coefficients are under one, so the chip can use a fine step, one over a hundred twenty-eight. Point eight one becomes a hundred and four steps, point eight one two five. The poles move out by point zero zero one four. That's all.
Now the three-notch rule. Its biggest coefficient is two point eight six. A step of one over sixty-four only reaches just under two, so the chip is forced to one over thirty-two, for every coefficient. Each lands about a hundredth off. Small, you'd think.
Find the rounded rule's poles. The middle pair moves in. The outer pairs jump outside the unit circle, to one point oh five seven, so an echo grows almost six percent per sample. The chip's rule isn't your rule. It's a neighbor, and that neighbor isn't stable.
But why? Next to lesson six, these errors are five and a half times bigger, yet the poles moved seventy to a hundred times as far. The book's answer: a pole moves by the error over the product of its distances to the other poles. One over that product is its multiplier.
Here, three poles are crammed together near the top. The middle one's neighbors are just point zero seven seven away. [surprise-oh] Its multiplier is twenty-two. Lesson six's lone pair sits one point eight apart, a multiplier of point five six. Its error shrinks.
Multiplying out crowds all six poles into one rule, each coefficient a blend of all of them. So don't multiply. Build one notch at a time: a second-order section, one zero pair and one pole pair, where each number is something you can see. Run three in a row. That's a cascade.
Each section is rounded alone, at a step of one over sixty-four. Every pole lands at point nine seven six, safely inside. The whine keeps one point three percent at most, the voice ninety-nine and a half. Tiny errors aren't always tiny trouble. Stability is a line, and crowded poles get pushed across it.
