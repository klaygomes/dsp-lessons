# The Chip That Hums

Objective: after this, you can round a filter's coefficients the way an 8-bit chip does, find where the rounded coefficients put the poles, explain why crowded poles move far more than lone ones (error × 1 / product of distances to the other poles), and build a fragile multiplied-out filter as a cascade of second-order sections, zeros, poles and volume factor included, that survives the chip.

Bug: the whine hops between 1,900, 2,000 and 2,100 Hz. Three notches at those tones, poles at radius 0.98, multiplied into one sixth-order rule, work on the laptop but hum and blow up on the 8-bit chip. The biggest coefficient (2.8576) forces a step of 1/32. Rounding moves two pole pairs to radius 1.0567 at 1,817 and 2,183 Hz, so a click grows 5.7% per sample (ringing at 1,817 and 2,183 Hz) until it hits the chip's limits within 10 to 20 ms; the clipped loop then swings between the two limits forever, a full-volume hum (after a click, a pattern repeating every 6 samples, near 1,333 Hz and 2,667 Hz). The input side also rounds to exactly 0.9375 (1 + z⁻²)³, so all three zero pairs collapse onto 2,000 Hz and the 1,900 / 2,100 Hz whines leak at 12.9%.
Fix: act 2 of the lab: build a cascade of three second-order sections, each rounded on its own at step 1/64. Every pole lands at radius 0.9763; the whine at 1,900, 2,000 and 2,100 Hz is at 1.3%, 0% and 1.3%; the voice at 1,500 Hz at 99.5%.

Honest variant (deviation from the series table): the table names lesson 6's notch y[n] = 0.905 (x[n] + x[n−2]) − 0.81 y[n−2]. With plain 8-bit rounding (step 1/128) its poles move from 0.9 to 0.9014 and nothing goes wrong; that is worked example 1. The blow-up needs crowded poles and big coefficients, so the bug filter is the three-notch rule. The table's fix (a cascade of second-order sections) only applies to a higher-order filter, which this variant supplies.

Key words: volume factor (defined in worked example 1: the number in front of a filter's input side, chosen so that 0 Hz comes out at exactly 100%), coefficient (not "weight", "tap", "parameter"), quantization (the noun) and round / rounded (the verb; not "truncate", "approximate"), step (not "resolution", "LSB", "precision"), the chip, the laptop, stored value, input side / feedback side (the two halves of a rule; top / bottom for the system function's fraction), multiplier (error × 1 / product of distances), steepness (of P at a root; "slope" once, "tangent" for the line), cascade (not "chain"), second-order section (not "biquad"), volume factor, the multiplied-out rule (not "direct form" in prose), the hum, pole, zero, unit circle, stable, notch, passes / removes, system function H(z).

Terms (new, as written in the prose):
- coefficient: a fixed number in a filter's rule that a sample is multiplied by.
- quantization: rounding a number to the nearest value the chip can store.
- second-order section: a filter that reaches back at most two samples on its input and its output, so it holds one pole pair and one zero pair.
- cascade: filters run one after another, each one's output feeding the next one's input.

Reused, with the earlier wording: filter, passes / removes, unit circle, zero, pole ("its radius is how much an echo keeps per sample"), notch, stable, system function H(z) ("the number a tone z^n is multiplied by when it passes through the filter", recapped in worked example 1 with top and bottom named), lesson 6's zero-pair recipe 1 − 2cos θ z⁻¹ + z⁻² (lesson 6); the mirror poles on the bottom half (lesson 6); clipping (lesson 1); the r = 0.85 notch (lesson 7); lesson 8's all-pass as another second-order section. New tool introduced in the prose: the gain at 0 Hz is the top's coefficient sum over the bottom's (put z = 1). The book is named in full at the derivation: Oppenheim and Schafer, Discrete-Time Signal Processing (1989), chapter 6.

Misconception: "Tiny rounding errors can only cause tiny trouble." · Why wrong: a pole moves by the error times 1 / (product of its distances to the other poles), which is 0.5 for a lone pair but 11 and 22 for these crowded poles; and stability is a line: 0.98 rings and dies, 1.0567 hums forever.

Worked examples:
1. Lesson 6's notch on the chip, step 1/128: 0.905 → 116/128 = 0.90625, 0.81 → 104/128 = 0.8125. Poles 0.9 → 0.9014 (0.0025 × 1/1.8 = 0.0014). Zeros stay at ±j. 1,500 Hz 96.9% → 97.0%, 1,900 Hz 60.0% → 60.5%, 0 Hz (0.90625 + 0.90625) ÷ (1 + 0.8125) = 1.
2. The three-notch rule on the chip, step 1/32: laptop feedback 2.8576, 2.7444, 0.8858 stored as 2.84375 (91), 2.75 (88), 0.875 (28), errors −0.0138, +0.0056, −0.0108; input side 0.9418 → 0.9375, 2.8021 → 2.8125. Poles 0.98 at 1,900 / 2,000 / 2,100 Hz → 1.0567 at 1,817 Hz, 0.8378 at 2,000 Hz, 1.0567 at 2,183 Hz.
Pencil section between them: the three feedback sides multiply to 1 + 2.8576 z⁻² + 2.7444 z⁻⁴ + 0.8858 z⁻⁶; the zero sides to 1 + 2.9754 z⁻² + 2.9754 z⁻⁴ + z⁻⁶; volume factor 0.9802³ = 0.9418; 0.9418 × 2.9754 = 2.8021.
Section build before "Your turn": 1,900 Hz zeros 1 − 0.1569 z⁻¹ + z⁻², poles 1 − 0.1538 z⁻¹ + 0.9604 z⁻², factor 1.8066 ÷ 1.8431 = 0.9802; program y[n] = 0.9802 (x[n] − 0.1569 x[n−1] + x[n−2]) + 0.1538 y[n−1] − 0.9604 y[n−2].

Derivation (chapter 6, pole sensitivity in the multiplied-out form), told with pictures, using the slope of a curve as its one calculus idea: (1) move feedback terms across the equals sign (sign flips) and multiply top and bottom by z⁶: P(z) = z⁶ + 2.8576 z⁴ + 2.7444 z² + 0.8858; (2) P is the product of six factors (z − p); (3) an error ε in one coefficient adds ε z⁴ (or z², 1), of size about ε near a pole; (4) tangent picture: the crossing slides by ε ÷ steepness; (5) the steepness at p₁ is the product of the five arrows from p₁ to the other poles (factor (z − p₁) out; the rest is nearly constant); (6) move ≈ error × 1 / product of distances, and the pushes from the three errors add like arrows. No ∏, no ≲, no product rule.

Lab:
- [[lab]] act 1, explore (after worked example 1): lesson 6's notch with a "laptop / chip" toggle and a step selector from 1/128 down to 1/2. The unit circle shows laptop poles (hollow) and chip poles (filled) live, with each stored whole number, the pole radius, and gains at 0, 1,500, 1,900, 2,000 Hz; a click button plays the ring. Prediction: "how coarse must the step be before this notch goes unstable?" Answer: never past the edge; at 1/4 and 1/8, 0.81 rounds to 0.75 (poles 0.866, stable); at 1/16, to 0.8125 (0.9014); only at a step of 1/2 does 0.81 round up to 1.0, putting the poles on the circle (a ring that never dies). A lone pair barely cares.
- [[lab-act2]] act 2, fix: the three-notch filter. Step 1: load the multiplied-out rule; the step is forced to 1/32; the poles cross the rim; a call plays and the hum builds until the samples pin (meter shows the level climbing and clipping; audio fades in from quiet to avoid a jump in volume). Step 2: switch to "cascade" and build three sections: for each, place a zero pair on a whine and a pole pair just inside at the same angle; the lab computes each volume factor and the chip rounds everything at step 1/64. Pass: every chip pole inside the circle, the whine at 1,900, 2,000 and 2,100 Hz each under 2%, the voice at 1,500 Hz above 95%. Reference: zeros at the three whines, poles at 0.98 → chip poles 0.9763, whine 1.3%, 0%, 1.3%, voice 99.5%.

Assumptions:
- The chip's format (8-bit signed whole numbers times one shared power-of-two step per rule, or per section) is a story choice matching common fixed-point practice. Rounding of samples and products is mentioned only for the pinned output (lesson 1's clipping) and for why the 9 to 12 bit cancellation fails on a real chip.
- Lesson 6 taught the zero-pair recipe 1 − 2cos θ z⁻¹ + z⁻² (after its step six); lesson 8 first states the pole version with r, and this lesson points back to it.
- The tangent idea (a curve near a root behaves like its tangent) is shown with a picture, not proved; the complex case is stated in one sentence.
- Lesson 7 left the line's notch at 0.85; the hopping whine and the three-notch filter replace it in this lesson's story.
- "Anti-aliasing filter" in the cliffhanger is the filter lesson 5 used before downsampling.
