A newspaper's tip line must disguise every voice, so you move every tone up three hundred hertz: you multiply every sample by a three hundred hertz tone.
Within the hour, the reporters call back: there's a second voice under every caller, just as loud, a little lower. Where did it come from? You only multiplied.
Multiply one tone, a thousand hertz, for eight samples. The product is half a thirteen hundred hertz tone plus half a seven hundred, sample for sample. The tone split in two: the upper half is the wanted copy, the lower half the ghost.
Filter it away? A voice runs from three hundred to thirty-four hundred hertz, so the ghosts land across the wanted copy: the ghost of two thousand sits at seventeen hundred, on the wanted fourteen hundred. No filter can tell them apart.
Back to lesson three's wheel: a tone is a dot turning counter-clockwise, and the line stores only its left-right shadow. Lesson six split a real tone into two half-size arrows: the top one counter-clockwise, its mirror clockwise. A clockwise dot is a negative tone, and sounds like its positive twin.
Multiplying arrows adds their turns: thirteen and a half degrees more per sample. The top arrow speeds up, to thirteen hundred hertz. The mirror turns the other way, so it slows down, to minus seven hundred, which sounds at seven hundred. The mirrors are the ghost.
So remove the mirrors first. A call made only of top arrows is the analytic signal. For one tone, that's a full-size dot: the cosine you store, plus the sine, its up-down part. The sine is the cosine a quarter turn behind.
The filter that makes it is the Hilbert transformer. It turns positive tones back a quarter turn and negative tones forward, so the line can still carry it. Turn that output one more quarter turn forward, and add the call. The top arrow lands on itself: doubled. The mirror lands opposite. [surprise-oh] Gone.
Now shift by one arrow, three hundred hertz: no mirrors to slow down. Keep the shadow: the call times the cosine, minus the Hilbert output times the sine. A thousand hertz comes out as thirteen hundred, digit for digit. One copy only: that's single sideband.
The Hilbert transformer's coefficients never end, like lesson ten's ideal filter. Keep thirty-one, taper them with a Hann window: it runs fifteen samples late. Delay the plain call to match, or the ghost is back: forty-one percent at a thousand hertz.
The target: every ghost under five percent, from three hundred to thirty-four hundred hertz. Thirty-one coefficients just miss: five point two four percent at three hundred. Thirty-five, with a delay of seventeen: two point seven seven. The ghost is gone.
