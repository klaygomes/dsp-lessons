In the basement sits the old analog exchange, and this month it gets switched off. Your boss wants the new digital line to sound exactly the same. The manual describes its line filter: a Butterworth low-pass, cutoff thirty-four hundred hertz, where it passes seventy-one percent. A colleague's script turns that into a digital filter, the textbook recipe.
Next morning, a technician plays tones through both. At one thousand hertz the copy is perfect. At thirty-four hundred, the exchange passes seventy-one percent, and your copy passes ten. Customers sound dull, as if the top of every voice was shaved off. So what does the recipe do to frequencies?
The exchange never sampled anything. Its sound is a voltage in a wire, changing smoothly, through a circuit of capacitors and coils. That's an analog filter. With no samples, there's no wheel and no limit: it has a response at every frequency, up to a million hertz and beyond.
So here's the mismatch. Analog frequencies live on a ruler that runs on forever. Digital tones live on half a circle, from zero to four thousand hertz. Copying means deciding where each mark of the ruler lands. Wrap it round and round, and five thousand lands on three thousand. That's aliasing. Or bend it on once, and squeeze.
That squeeze is the bilinear transform. Near zero, the ruler lands almost one for one: five hundred hertz lands on four ninety-four. Further up it squeezes harder, and infinity lands exactly on four thousand. That uneven squeeze is warping. And thirty-four hundred lands on twenty-three sixty-three. There's your bug, in one row.
The squeeze never changes a gain. It only moves gains to new tones. So the copy passes seventy-one percent, exactly, at the tone where thirty-four hundred landed. Every gain is right, at the wrong tone. And once you know where each one lands, you can aim.
Run the squeeze backward. Which analog frequency lands on thirty-four hundred? Forty-four hundred? Five thousand? Start at thirty-four hundred on the digital side, go across to the curve, then down to the analog frequency. [surprise-oh] Ten thousand six hundred seven. More than three times higher, far above anything the line can carry. That's prewarping.
Build the analog prototype, the filter you copy, with its cutoff there. Then squeeze. Its whole stretch up to the cutoff lands below thirty-four hundred, and that stretch is nearly flat. The technician measures again: seventy point seven percent at thirty-four hundred hertz. You changed one number. That's the whole fix.
Prewarping pins one tone, and every other tone is still squeezed. But the cutoff is the tone worth pinning: below it, the copy never strays more than one point six decibels.
