# The Detector That Couldn't Keep Up

Objective: after this, you can explain why bins k and k + N ÷ 2 share their work, compute a butterfly by hand, count the multiplications of a direct DFT (2 × N × N) and an FFT ((N ÷ 2) × log₂ N butterflies at 4 each), choose a power-of-two block that still separates the keypad tones, derive Goertzel's recurrence from a pole pair, and say when Goertzel beats the FFT.

Bug: to reject voices that fake a key, the detector computes bins 0 to 102 of every 205-sample block: 42,230 multiplications per block, 1.65 million a second, and the cheap chip from lesson 9 manages about 1 million. It falls behind live calls.
Fix: act 2 of the lab: switch the block to N = 256, then split the DFT into even and odd samples round by round until the multiplications per block fit the 32,000 budget (3 splits), and all the way to the FFT's 4,096; the detector then decodes live key presses against the lab's 25% bar. The live line's 12% bar and its rejections are left for lesson 14.

Key words: multiplication (the one cost unit; "turn" only ever means a rotation), turn back / back = clockwise, split (not "decimate" in prose), even part / odd part, butterfly, layer, position, FFT, bit-reversed order, Goertzel, cos (the left-right part of an arrow, named in the Goertzel section), test wheel, bin, match, bar, block, frequency resolution (lesson 12 meanings).

Terms:
- butterfly: takes the odd result and turns it by one arrow of the test wheel, then adds the turned result to the even result for bin k and subtracts it for bin k + N ÷ 2; four multiplications.
- FFT: the DFT split into even and odd samples again and again until only butterflies are left; (N ÷ 2) × log₂ N butterflies, 2 × N × log₂ N multiplications, against 2 × N × N.
- Goertzel: a filter with a pole pair on the unit circle at one bin's point, run for N samples, whose last two outputs give that one bin.
- Helper words, defined in place: multiplication as the cost unit (a sample into an arrow costs 2, an arrow turned by an arrow costs 4), even part, odd part, layer (all the butterflies that combine small DFTs of one size into DFTs twice that size), position (one of the N places the numbers sit in between layers), bit-reversed order, cos θ (the left-right part of the arrow at angle θ).

Reused terms: DFT, bin, match, test wheel, frequency resolution, DTMF, bar (lesson 12, 25% there; the live line uses 12%); filter, pole, zero, mirror arrow, unit circle, stable, H(z) (lesson 6); the 8-bit chip (lesson 9).

Misconception: "The FFT is a faster, rougher approximation of the DFT (or one with better resolution)." Why wrong: it computes exactly the same N numbers in a different order; on 256 random samples the two differ by about 1.8 × 10⁻¹², rounding only, and the bins and the resolution 8,000 ÷ N are unchanged.

Worked examples:
1. N = 8, bins 1 and 5: clockwise angles 0, 45, 90, ... 315° and 0, 225, 90, 315, 180, 45, 270, 135°; equal on even samples, half a turn apart on odd ones. Recording 1, 1, 0, −1, −1, −1, 0, 1: even part (1, 0, −1, 0) gives 2 at bin 1 of a 4-sample DFT; odd part (1, −1, −1, 1) gives 2 right + 2 up = 2.83 at 45°, turned back 45° gives 2.83 right; bin 1 = 4.83, bin 5 = −0.83, matching the direct DFT.
2. The rule stretches: N = 205 = 5 × 41 cannot be halved, so N = 256: resolution 31.25 Hz, 32 ms, keypad tones on bins 22, 25, 27, 30, 39, 43, 47, 52. Multiplications per block after 0, 1, 3, 8 splits (shown in the table): 131,072, 66,048, 17,920, 4,096; the budget figure shows all nine rows (also 33,792, 10,240, 6,656, 5,120, 4,608). Rule: each small DFT 2 × size × size, a one-sample DFT 0, plus 128 butterflies × 4 per layer. Budget 32,000: 3 splits fit, 2 do not.

Derivation (chapter 9, decimation in time), framed as the general rule for every N and every k. On the page the section opens with the two butterfly lines and the rule (compute E and O once, each turn serves bins k and k + N/2) and the butterfly count B(N); the five steps and the wrap-wheel figure sit in a closed aside, "Show the five steps from the book": split X[k] into n = 2r and n = 2r + 1 (r counts the pairs); pull out W_N^k; W_N² = W_{N/2}, so X[k] = E[k] + W_N^k O[k]; the wrap fact (bin k + N/2 of an N/2-sample DFT turns a whole extra turn per sample), shown with a 4-sample wheel, so E[k + N/2] = E[k]; W_N^{k+N/2} = −W_N^k gives the butterfly; each turn W_N^k O[k] serves bins k and k + N/2; B(N) = 2B(N/2) + N/2, B(1) = 0, so B(N) = (N/2) log₂ N. Book's G[k], H[k] renamed E[k], O[k].
Second derivation (Goertzel; on the page the main thread keeps the idea, a one-line lesson 6 reminder of H(z), z⁻¹, pole and zero, the recurrence, the swing figure, the last step with |X[k]| glossed as a length, and the cost comparison; the H(z) multiplication and the "why the last step is the bin" argument with the goertzel-final figure sit in two closed "Show ..." asides): (1 − p z⁻¹)(1 − p̄ z⁻¹) = 1 − (p + p̄) z⁻¹ + p p̄ z⁻²; p + p̄ = 2 cos θ (up-down parts cancel), p p̄ = 1 (opposite angles); read as feedback: s[n] = x[n] + 2cos(2πk/N) s[n−1] − s[n−2]. Final step y = s[N−1] − W_N^k s[N−2] adds a zero on the mirror pole, leaving one pole at p, whose output at N − 1 is every sample turned forward by its age, which is the DFT walk turned forward by N − 1 steps as a whole: same length.

Assumptions:
- The cost unit is the ordinary multiplication throughout (the book counts complex multiplications, which are the butterflies here); trivial turns (by 1 or −1) are counted. Bin lengths (squares) are not counted for any method.
- The chip does about 1,000,000 multiplications a second: a story fact shared with lesson 12.
- The row for no splits counts all 256 bins; the prose notes the mirror-skipping direct DFT (66,048) and that real-input FFTs halve the same way.
- Lesson 6 taught that poles on the unit circle ring forever and are not stable, that a real filter's poles and zeros come in mirror pairs, and how to read a system function's bottom as feedback (its notch example). Goertzel uses all three.
- "cos" is named here for the first time as the left-right part of an arrow; lesson 6 used it in formulas.
- Under the bar rule a 4 is never decoded as a 7: the press is thrown away as "4 and 7 at once". Lesson 14's bug wording should be read that way.
