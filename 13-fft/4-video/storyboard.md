# Storyboard: The Detector That Couldn't Keep Up

**Problem:** the keypad detector from lesson 12 computes every bin of every 205-sample block (103 bins × 205 samples × 2 = 42,230 multiplications a block, 1.65 million a second), and the cheap chip manages about 1 million a second, so it falls behind live calls. **Audience:** software engineers with no signal processing background who did lessons 1 to 12. **Goal:** the viewer can say that in a block of 8 the test wheels of bins 1 and 5 point the same way on even samples and opposite on odd ones; that the even part and the odd part, each added up once, give bin 1 = E + O and bin 5 = E − O; work the hand example (recording 1, 1, 0, −1, −1, −1, 0, 1: even part 2, odd walk 2 right and 2 up, turned back 45° to 2.83, so bin 1 = 4.83 and bin 5 = −0.83); name that step a butterfly (four multiplications); say that splitting again and again leaves only butterflies (three layers of four for 8 samples, 48 multiplications instead of 128), which is the FFT; that the FFT needs a power-of-two block, so 205 (5 × 41) becomes 256, where every keypad tone still has its own bin and the budget is 32,000 a block; that 3 splits fit (17,920) and 2 do not (33,792), and the full FFT needs 4,096; that the FFT gives exactly the DFT's bins; and that one bin alone is cheaper with Goertzel (bin 20 of key 5: 91.8). **Emotion:** from "the line is falling behind" through the small surprise of two bins sharing their arrows, to the relief of the bill dropping under the chip's line, then the sigh of a new complaint.
