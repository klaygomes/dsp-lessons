The keypad detector from the last lesson works, and to reject voices that fake a key, it computes every bin of every block. Then you put it on the cheap chip. Forty-two thousand multiplications a block, one point six five million a second, and the chip manages about a million. It falls behind.
Is the DFT really doing forty-two thousand different things? Take a block of eight. Bin one's test wheel turns back forty-five degrees a sample, bin five's two hundred and twenty-five. On the even samples they point the same way. On the odd ones, exactly opposite.
So add up the turned even samples once: the even part. Then the odd ones: the odd part. Bin one is even plus odd, bin five is even minus odd. Two bins for the price of one.
Try a rough one thousand hertz tone: one, one, zero, minus one, minus one, minus one, zero, one. The even part is two, pointing right. The odd samples walk two right and two up, but each sits one sample later, so turn the walk back one more step: two point eight three, pointing right.
Bin one is two plus two point eight three: four point eight three. Bin five is two minus two point eight three: minus point eight three. Turn, add, subtract: that step is a butterfly, four multiplications.
Now the dramatic move. A four-sample DFT is a DFT, so split it too, and again, down to single samples, which cost nothing. Only butterflies are left, three layers of four: forty-eight multiplications instead of a hundred and twenty-eight. That's the FFT, the fast Fourier transform.
Back to the line. The FFT halves the block again and again, so its length must be a power of two, and two hundred and five is five times forty-one. Take two hundred and fifty-six: every keypad tone still gets its own bin, and the chip can spend thirty-two thousand a block.
Now split, and watch the bill. No splits: a hundred and thirty-one thousand. One: sixty-six thousand. Two: thirty-four thousand, still over. [surprise-oh] Three, and it fits. All the way down, the FFT needs four thousand and ninety-six, an eighth of the chip.
Your turn: pick the block, add splits until the meter fits, and decode a caller's digits live. The FFT is not a rougher DFT: it gives exactly the same bins, still thirty-one point two five hertz apart, just fast enough.
Need only a few bins? Goertzel is a filter ringing at one bin's point. For bin twenty of key five, its last two outputs give ninety-one point eight, the DFT's match, at one multiplication a sample. Under about sixteen bins, it beats the FFT.
